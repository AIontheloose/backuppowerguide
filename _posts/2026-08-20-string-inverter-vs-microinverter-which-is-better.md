## String Inverter vs Microinverter – Which Is Better in 2026?

When I first started commissioning rooftop solar systems across New South Wales, the conversation revolved around panel wattage and mounting rails. Today, the real engineering challenge sits squarely on the wall behind your meter box. With over 25% of Australian households now generating their own electricity, choosing between a string inverter and a microinverter is no longer just a budget decision—it’s a system architecture choice that dictates how long your setup will last, how much power you actually harvest, and how easily it will integrate with tomorrow’s grid.

I’ve spent the past decade running shading analyses on corrugated iron in Queensland, diagnosing fault codes in Melbourne attics, and watching hybrid battery adoption spike across Perth. The market has matured, but so have the trade-offs. Let’s cut through the marketing and look at what actually matters when you’re balancing upfront cost, long-term yield, and future expansion.

### 1. Energy Conversion Efficiency – What the Numbers Really Mean

Efficiency ratings dominate spec sheets, but raw percentages rarely tell the whole story. String inverters typically boast **98.5% maximum efficiency**, while microinverters sit in the **96.0–97.2%** range. On paper, that gap looks negligible. In practice, it’s about where and how power is converted.

| Inverter Type | Typical Efficiency | AUD 2026 Retail Price | Real-World Context |
|---------------|-------------------|------------------------|---------------------|
| String Inverter | ~98.5% | $1,180–$1,260 (10 kW) | Centralised conversion; single point of failure |
| Microinverter | 96.0–97.2% | $270–$290 (per unit) | Panel-level DC-to-AC conversion; independent operation |

On a standard 10 kW array, that 1.3–2.5% efficiency differential translates to roughly **300 Wh of daily energy loss** over the system’s lifespan. At current Australian electricity rates (~$0.30 AUD/kWh), you’re looking at approximately **$200–$250 less annual savings** if you prioritise microinverters solely on conversion metrics. However, efficiency is only half the equation. Where those watts are harvested matters far more than how efficiently they’re converted.

### 2. Cost Breakdown & Installation Labour – The Hidden Margins

Upfront hardware costs tell one story; labour tells another. Microinverters require individual mounting, DC wiring, and AC combiner connections on every rafter or rail segment. String inverters consolidate that complexity into a single wall-mounted enclosure.

| Component / Service | String Inverter (10 kW) | Microinverter (10 kW / ~27 panels) |
|---------------------|--------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| Hardware Cost (AUD) | $1,220 | $7,560 (~$280 × 27) |
| Installation Labour (AUD) | $450–$600 | $900–$1,350 |
| **Total System Hardware + Labour** | **$1,670–$1,820** | **$8,460–$8,910** |

Labour rates vary by state due to tradie demand and regional compliance requirements. In NSW and VIC, certified installers typically charge $85–$110/hour. Queensland’s hotter climate often demands additional thermal clearances, pushing microinverter labour closer to the upper bound. If you’re working with a tight cash flow or deploying on a straightforward, unobstructed roof, the string inverter remains the financially sensible default. But if your site presents real-world complications, that hardware premium quickly pays for itself through avoided yield loss.

### 3. Shading Tolerance & Real-World Performance

Shading is the silent killer of centralised systems. When a single panel in a series string gets dappled by eucalyptus leaves or a neighbour’s chimney, the entire string’s output drops to match that weakest link.
