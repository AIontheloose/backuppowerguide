## How to Replace Brake Pads at Home (Australia 2026)

In 2026, the average front brake pad set for a mid-size sedan or light ute in Australia costs around AUD $138, but hand it off to a local workshop and you’re staring down roughly $78 an hour just for labour. Tally that up with the typical billed time, shop overheads, and parts markups, and a professional front brake job on a 2024 Toyota Hilux or Holden Commodore routinely lands between $1,100 and $1,350. DIY suddenly stops feeling like a weekend chore and starts looking like a genuinely smart financial move. I’ve been wrenching on everything from Melbourne’s gridlocked commuters to the dust-choked highways of Western Australia for over a decade, and let me tell you: swapping brake pads is one of the most rewarding maintenance jobs you can tackle in your own driveway. It requires patience, basic tool literacy, and a healthy respect for safety, but it also gives you direct control over stopping power, compound selection, and long-term ownership costs.

### Why DIY Brake Pad Replacement Makes Sense Now

Let’s be honest about the numbers without the sales pitch. When you factor in parts, fluid top-ups, and workshop administrative fees, a typical shop in Sydney or Brisbane will bill you around $1,200 for a full front-set on a modern V6 ute. Compare that to roughly $138 for the pads alone, plus a can of cleaner and a few consumables like grease or wire brush heads. The savings are immediate, but the real value lies in what you gain as a car owner: you learn exactly what your calipers are doing, spot seized guide pins before they become expensive repairs, and avoid the dreaded “new pad squeal” that happens when shops skip proper bedding-in procedures. I recommend tackling this job if you’re already comfortable using a torque wrench, reading a service manual, and working on level ground. The upfront time investment pays for itself within two to three service intervals.

### Step-by-Step: The Brake Pad Replacement Steps

1. **Secure the vehicle.** Park on level ground, engage the handbrake, and chock the rear wheels. Lower the vehicle with your hydraulic jack until the tyre is just off the ground, then immediately place jack stands under the manufacturer’s designated lift points. Double-check stability by bouncing the car firmly before crawling underneath.
2. **Remove the wheel.** Most modern Australian cars use 19mm or 21mm sockets for lug nuts. Work in a star pattern to avoid warping the rotor. Set the wheel aside and prop the car up securely on the stands.
3. **Access the caliper.** Locate the two brake pad guide pins on the back of the caliper bracket. These are usually 14mm or 17mm bolts. Remove them carefully; if they’re corroded, apply penetrating oil and let it soak for ten minutes. Never force them with a hammer.
4. **Slide the caliper off.** Rest it on a hook or hang it from the suspension spring with a bungee cord. Do not let it dangle by the brake hose, as this can damage the flexible line or introduce air into the system.
5. **Remove old pads and inspect hardware.** Slide the worn pads out of the bracket. Check the guide pins for smooth movement; if they’re sticky, clean them with brake cleaner and apply a thin layer of silicone-based caliper grease. Replace any anti-rattle clips or shims that are bent or corroded.
6. **Compress the caliper piston.** Use your piston tool to slowly push the piston back into its bore until it’s fully retracted. Keep an eye on your brake fluid reservoir under the bonnet; as the piston retreats, fluid level will rise. If it’s too full, siphon some out with a turkey baster to prevent overflow when you reinstall the caliper.
7. **Install new pads and rebuild.** Slide the new pads into the bracket (apply a hair-thin layer of ceramic brake grease to the back contact points only, never the friction surface). Reinstall guide pins and torque them to your vehicle’s spec (usually 25–35 Nm for most sedans, but always check the manual). Mount the caliper back over the pads, reinstall the wheel, lower the car, and torque the lug nuts in a star pattern.

> **ABS & ESC Note:** If your vehicle is ABS-equipped, a single-axis pad change can occasionally trigger the ABS warning light due to minor hydraulic pressure imbalances. Always check the warning panel after re-installation. A quick ignition cycle or a short drive usually clears it, but if the light persists, run a diagnostic scan.

#### Bleeding the brakes
If you notice a spon

gy pedal feel, or if you've had the caliper off the car for any length of time, air has likely crept into the lines. Here's how to fix it:

**Bleeding the brakes**
Clean the bleeder valve on the caliper before you touch it; road grit is your enemy and will inject itself straight into your hydraulic system if you don't. Attach a clear vinyl tube to the bleeder nipple and drop the free end into a quart jar containing a bit of fresh brake fluid. You can run solo with a one-way vacuum kit, but two people work fastest and safest.

Have a helper pump the pedal firmly three times and hold it down. Crack the bleeder valve open. Fluid will surge out; close the valve *before* your helper releases the pedal. Repeat until the stream is bubble-free and solid. **Critical Rule:** Never let the master cylinder reservoir run dry during this process. If that drops below the minimum line, you suck air back into the main pump, and now you're bleeding for hours. Top off with fresh fluid as you go, every step of the way.

#### FAQ: Dan's Quick-Reference Guide

**Q: Can I mix different brands of brake pads?**
**Dan's Answer:** Never play mechanic roulette. Mix friction compounds, and your wear rates will mismatch, causing the vehicle to pull hard under braking or creating uneven heat buildup. Stick to one high-quality set per axle.

**Q: Do new pads need "bedding in"?**
**Dan's Answer:** Absolutely. Bedding transfers a thin, even layer of pad material onto the rotor, which is essential for maximum grip and silence. Follow the manufacturer's transfer protocol—usually a series of controlled moderate stops followed by cool-downs. Skip this, and you risk glazing, squeal, or reduced stopping power.

**Q: How do I know if my rotors are warped?**
**Dan's Answer:** True warping is rare; usually, it's just roughness or thickness variation (DTV). If your steering wheel shakes at highway speeds when braking, measure rotor runout with a dial indicator. If it's out of spec, replace or machine the rotors. Don't blame the pads for bad rotors.

**Q: Can I use DOT 5 silicone fluid in an ABS car?**
**Dan's Answer:** No. DOT 5 is non-hygroscopic and compressible under pressure, which can cause severe brake fade and damage sensitive ABS pump seals. Stick to DOT 3 or DOT 4 glycol-based fluids unless your manual explicitly says otherwise.
