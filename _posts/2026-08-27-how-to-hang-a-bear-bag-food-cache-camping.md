## How to Hang a Bear-Bag Food Cache While Camping

Right in the heart of the Blue Mountains this year, rangers have logged a sharp uptick in food storage violations compared to previous seasons. It's not just a statistic; it's a red flag waving at every camper thinking they can get away with leaving a snag packet on a table and walking off into the bush. In my experience chasing ridgelines from Kosciuszko to Cape York over the last decade, I've learned one hard truth: the Australian landscape doesn't care about your picnic plans if you leave food unsecured. We're in 2026 now. Wildlife management is tighter, regulations are stricter, and the bush is more unpredictable than ever due to shifting climate patterns. Whether you're camping near Sydney's coastal ranges or deep in the Kimberley, hanging a bear bag isn't just good practice; for many of us, it's the law. This guide cuts through the fluff. I'm Jake Morrison, and I'm going to show you how to secure your grub, protect your gear, and keep the local wildlife happy—because a fed animal is often an angry one.

### The Australian Reality: Why Food Storage Matters Now

Forget what you know from American movies about grizzlies. Here in Oz, the threats are different but just as destructive. In NSW, the *National Parks and Wildlife Act 1974* (specifically Section 23(2) regarding the protection of wildlife and public safety) mandates proper food storage when camping in high-traffic areas like the Blue Mountains. Violations can see you fined on the spot or banned from parks. But even if you're where regulations aren't explicitly enforced, common sense must rule.

I've seen camps raided by a single curious pademelon that figured out how to pull down a poorly tied line. Kangaroos have thick skulls and powerful jaws; they'll strip canvas and tear zippers without hesitation. Possums are intelligent climbers with nimble fingers that can undo the simplest knots, while curlews and crows will pick apart an unsecured pack in minutes. Beyond the damage to your gear, feeding wildlife creates habituated animals that eventually need to be euthanised for public safety. Secure your food; it's the right thing to do.

Test the hang with a firm tug before dark; if it sways more than a few inches, adjust your anchor or shorten the span.

**Frequently Asked Questions**

*Do I need a permit for food hangs in my area?* Many national forests and parks require bear canisters instead of bags due to ground-squirrel or raccoon issues. Always check local land management rules before relying on a hang system.

*What’s the difference between a PCT-style hang and an Ursack?* The PCT method uses elevation and tension to keep food out of reach, while an Ursack relies on Kevlar® lining to resist punctures. Both are valid, but the PCT setup is significantly lighter and easier to pack for long-distance trips.

*How do I know when my rope or carabiner needs replacing?* Replace any line that shows glazing, stiffening, or frayed fibers from UV exposure. Swap out carabiners if the gate action becomes gritty or the nose wears down past 2mm. Never compromise on load-bearing hardware.

*Can I hang food without a counterweight?* Technically yes, but wind and temperature changes will cause sagging that brings your bag within reach of curious paws or rodents. The weighted clip is cheap insurance for consistent tension.

*Is ripstop nylon enough for long-term durability?* Only if it’s coated and double-stitched at the seams. Uncoated ripstop absorbs moisture and degrades faster. For multi-season use, look for PU-treated fabric with reinforced drawcord channels.

**Conclusion**

A properly engineered food hang system isn’t just regulatory compliance—it’s the difference between a peaceful night and a frantic dawn. Every piece in this setup works together: the 30m rope reaches high anchors without sacrificing strength, the weighted carabiner neutralizes environmental shifts, and a rated cache bag keeps contents dry and secure. Field experience teaches one thing above all: consistency beats convenience every time. Inspect your gear before each trip, store it clean and dry, and never assume a familiar campground is “bear-safe” enough to skip the hang. When you respect the systems that protect both you and local wildlife, you’re not just following rules—you’re preserving the integrity of the backcountry for everyone who follows. Pack smart, hang high, and let your preparation speak for itself when the trail gets remote.
