## Core Mechanics and Data Overview

### Low Expense Ratios and Cost Efficiency
The cost drag on your portfolio can silently erode returns over decades. In my analysis of the 2025-26 data, Betashares has maintained competitive pricing. The average expense ratio of **0.35%** is well below many active management counterparts. However, investors must note the variance; while broad market ETFs sit near the lower end at **0.20%**, specialised thematic funds like the Global Sustainable Energy ETF may carry fees up to **0.60%**.

When compounding over a 30-year superannuation horizon, that 0.40% spread can result in tens of thousands of dollars in difference. I always advise calculating the total cost of ownership, including brokerage, but the expense ratio remains the primary variable you cannot control once invested.

### Tax Efficiency and Franking Credits
One of the distinct advantages for Australians holding ASX-listed ETFs is the tax structure. These funds are taxed at the corporate level, and dividends distributed to investors often carry **franking credits at 30%**. This mechanism can significantly reduce your marginal tax liability.

For example, if you are in the top tax bracket, the franking credit acts as a tax offset. However, this benefit is contingent on the fund generating eligible Australian corporate profits. Overseas-listed ETFs or those with significant international revenue exposure may have lower franking coverage, which I factor heavily into my tax modelling for high-income earners.

### Superannuation Compatibility
ETFs are highly compatible with both Self-Managed Super Funds (SMSFs) and industry funds. A critical data point for 2026 is that there are typically **no extra fees** imposed by the super fund for holding ETFs beyond the ETF's own expense ratio. This makes them an attractive alternative to traditional managed funds where management fees can layer up. ## Strategic Implementation for Australian Portfolios

### Building a Diversified Core
For most investors, I recommend a core-satellite approach. The core should be broad-market ETFs like **A200** or **IOZ**. While A200 tracks the Australian 200, providing instant diversification across 200+ companies, IOZ focuses on the top 50. In my experience, IOZ can offer higher concentration risk; if your portfolio relies heavily on IOZ, you are effectively betting on a handful of large-cap names rather than the broader market breadth.

If you're starting with zero capital, discipline is key. I recommend reviewing Pay Yourself First: The Definitive 2026 Guide for Australian Savers" to lock in your contribution rhythm before evaluating ETF structures. Consistency beats timing the market every time. Once your automatic deposits are running, layer in your satellite positions carefully. Allocate no more than 20–30% to targeted exposures—whether that’s global equities, infrastructure, or sector-specific themes—and keep the remainder in low-cost, broadly diversified core funds. Rebalance annually or when any allocation drifts more than 5% from its target weight. Remember to leverage dividend reinvestment plans (DRPs) where available and hold investments for at least twelve months to qualify for Australia’s 33.3% capital gains tax discount.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I hedge my international ETF exposure?**
Hedging can reduce short-term volatility when the AUD strengthens, but it also introduces management fees, tracking error, and roll costs. For long-term investors, unhedged global ETFs typically deliver superior risk-adjusted returns over 5+ year horizons. Only consider hedged variants if you have a specific short-to-medium term cash flow need or want to neutralise currency risk for a defined period.

**How much capital do I actually need to start investing in Australian ETFs?**
Technically, you only need the price of one unit plus brokerage fees. Most brokers now offer $0 brokerage on ETF trades, meaning you can start with as little as $10–$50 per month through dollar-cost averaging. The real barrier isn’t capital—it’s consistency and a long-term mindset.

**What’s the difference between A200 and IOZ, and which is better for beginners?**
A200 tracks the S&P/ASX 200, giving you instant diversification across 200+ companies. IOZ focuses on the top 50, creating a heavily weighted portfolio dominated by financials, miners, and energy. Beginners should generally start with A200 for broader market exposure and lower concentration risk, moving to IOZ only if they understand the sector implications.

**How often should I rebalance my core-satellite portfolio?**
Annually is a practical benchmark, but trigger-based rebalancing works better in volatile markets. Rebalance when any single asset class exceeds or falls below its target allocation by more than 5%, or when your investment thesis for a satellite holding changes.

**Are there tax advantages to holding Australian vs. global ETFs?**
Both qualify for the 33.3% CGT discount if held over 12 months. However, Australian-listed ETFs with significant domestic exposure may offer franked dividends, which can be highly tax-effective for residents in higher tax brackets. Global ETFs typically pay unfranked foreign dividends, but they provide crucial currency diversification that often outweighs short-term tax differences.
