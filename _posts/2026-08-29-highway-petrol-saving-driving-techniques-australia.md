If you've been staring at the numbers lately and wincing, you're not alone.

As of July 2026, the Australian Energy Regulator (AER) reports the national average for 92-octane unleaded petrol at **$1.68/L**, while diesel has crept up to **$1.71/L**. These aren't just digits on a dashboard; they're real dollars vanishing into the exhaust when you're clocking thousands of kilometres on the Pacific Highway or the long, flat stretches out west. In my time as an automotive contributor who's spent more time tracking fuel flow rates than I care to admit, saving petrol on the highway isn't about buying magic gadgets or praying for lower pump prices. It's about understanding vehicle physics, mastering smooth inputs, and keeping your machine in honest working order. Let's cut through the marketing noise and look at what actually moves the needle for Aussie drivers in 2026.

### The Reality of Highway Fuel Costs: Baseline Numbers Matter

Before we talk about throttle discipline, you need to know your baseline. Not all vehicles burn fuel at the same rate, and highway driving magnifies those differences due to aerodynamic drag. Based on ADR 81/02 test cycles adjusted for real-world highway variance, let's look at the books.

Take a typical hybrid compact like the Toyota Corolla Hybrid. On mixed cycles, it averages around 4.2 L/100 km in real-world highway duty. At current pump prices, that translates to roughly $7.06 per 100 km. Compare that to a loaded 4‑WD pickup like the Ford Ranger Wildtrak, which typically drinks about 9.8 L/100 km under similar conditions, pushing your cost to approximately $16.56 per 100 km. That's a difference of nearly **$9.50 every century of travel**. Over a 10,000 km road trip, that's almost $950 vanishing into the exhaust for no measurable gain in productivity.

What I've found is that most drivers don't realise how much their vehicle choice and load configuration dictate baseline consumption. Aerodynamics matter more at highway speeds than city driving ever will. Once you push past 80 km/h, air resistance becomes your primary enemy. A roof rack left on after camping season, a spare tyre mounted on the back door, or even a slightly open sunroof can increase drag coefficients enough to ruin your fuel economy. If you're planning extended road trips this year, make sure you've done your homework on load management before you hit the bitumen. ### Driving Techniques That Actually Work

#### Smooth Throttle and Early Coasting

The single most effective highway fuel-saving technique is also the hardest to master: smooth, progressive throttle application. Australian highways are notorious for sudden traffic flow changes, speed camera zones, and wildlife crossings that force abrupt braking. Every time you slam the brakes, you're throwing away kinetic energy that cost petrol to generate. The fix is predictive driving. Scan 50–100 metres ahead, ease off the accelerator early, and let engine braking do the work until you need to tap the brake gently.

Modern fuel-injected engines cut fuel supply entirely when coasting in gear above idle RPM. However, there's a nuance here for manual versus automatic drivers:
*   **Automatics:** Most modern ECUs cut fuel instantly when off-throttle and vehicle speed is above 80 km/h. You can leave the car in *Drive* and coast freely on descents without worrying about lugging or fuel injection logic.
*   **Manuals:** Fuel cut-off typically engages between 1,200 and 1,500 RPM. If you're slowing down rapidly, keep the clutch engaged to maintain RPM above this threshold. If the RPM drops below idle, the ECU will inject fuel to prevent stalling, negating the savings.

> **Pro Tip:** On long descents common on routes like the Great Ocean Road or the Blue Mountains approaches, leave your car in drive (or your preferred gear) and coast at highway speed rather than riding the brakes. Modern ECUs manage this perfectly, and you'll save fuel while preserving brake pad life on corrugated mountain passes.

#### Speed Management and the 80–90 km/h Sweet Spot

Aerodynamic drag increases exponentially with speed. Going from 100 km/h to 120 km/h doesn't just increase your journey time by 20%; it can spike fuel consumption by 15–20% depending on your vehicle's shape and underbody airflow. I recommend dialing your cruise control into the **80–90 km/h bracket** whenever road conditions, safety, and traffic flow allow. On open stretches like the Mitchell or the Eyre Highway, holding 85 km/h consistently will shave noticeable litres off your tank without meaningfully extending travel time.

Just remember that Australian highway design prioritises consistent flow over raw speed, so patience pays dividends at the pump. If you're towing or carrying a full load of passengers, bump up to 90–95 km/h but avoid the temptation to chase 110+ km/h on long straights unless absolutely necessary.

### Road-Specific and Seasonal Factors

#### Hungry Stretches and Wind Profiles
Not all highways are created equal when it comes to fuel economy. The **Great Pacific Highway (B1)** north of Coffs Harbour hits you with crosswinds and long straights that tempt speedsters—that's where drag bites hardest. Similarly, the **Goulburn Valley Freeway** has those relentless headwinds coming off the plains that can add 0

...5 to 1.8 litres per 100km depending on your vehicle’s frontal area and ride height. If you’re driving a ute, dual-cab, or anything with a boxy rear end, that wind resistance isn’t just an annoyance—it’s a fuel guinea pig. Close your windows at speed, ditch the roof rack if it’s empty, and keep your undercarriage clear of mud flaps that aren’t properly aligned. A clean, smooth underside lets air flow efficiently instead of creating turbulence that drags you backward like a shopping trolley with a bent wheel.

Seasonal shifts matter just as much. Summer’s heat thins engine oil slightly and can push under-tyre pressures if you’re not monitoring them—every 5 kPa drop costs you roughly 0.3% in rolling resistance. Winter brings cold starts that keep your engine running rich for longer, so a three-minute idle before moving off isn’t just polite motoring; it’s economic sense. And when the wet season rolls in and roads turn slick, remember that smooth throttle inputs aren’t just about traction—they’re about keeping your fuel map from flooding with unnecessary revs. Keep your tyres properly inflated for the load, avoid standing water where possible, and let off the pedal early on descents to use engine braking instead of burning fuel to maintain speed uphill again.

### FAQ: Fuel Economy on Australian Highways

**Q: What’s the absolute sweet spot for highway fuel economy?**  
A: For most modern petrol and diesel vehicles, 85–90 km/h is the economic sweet spot. Beyond that, aerodynamic drag climbs exponentially. If your car’s manual cites a specific “cruise speed” for lowest consumption, trust it—but don’t obsess over hitting it exactly. Consistency beats perfection.

**Q: Does towing always wreck fuel economy?**  
A: Not always, but it changes the math. Add a trailer, and you’re fighting both weight and wind. Keep speeds at 85 km/h if possible, use lower gears to avoid lugging, and distribute load low and forward. A properly balanced tow rig will still outperform a poorly packed car every time.

**Q: Should I worry about tire pressure on long hauls?**  
A: Absolutely. Under-inflated tires increase rolling resistance and heat up faster, which degrades rubber and wastes fuel. Check pressures cold before departure, and add 10–20 kPa if you’re fully loaded or towing. Recheck halfway through your trip—heat expansion is real.

**Q: Is cruise control good for saving fuel?**  
A: On flat, predictable highways? Yes. It keeps your throttle steady and prevents micro-accelerations that spike consumption. But on rolling terrain or in strong crosswinds, manual modulation often wins. Let your right foot read the road when conditions change.

**Q: When should I just ignore fuel economy and drive faster?**  
A: When safety, traffic flow, or time pressure demands it. Fuel savings from driving 10 km/h slower are rarely worth a collision risk or stranding yourself in low-visibility conditions. Economy is a tool, not a rulebook.

### Conclusion

Fuel economy isn’t about chasing perfect numbers on a dashboard or turning your highway run into a fuel-saving experiment. It’s about understanding how your vehicle interacts with the road, the weather, and the load you’re carrying—and making decisions that keep you moving efficiently without compromising safety or sanity. Australian highways reward patience, smooth inputs, and respect for physics over pride. Tweak your speed, check your pressures, manage your drag, and let the landscape work with you instead of against you. Drive smart, save a few litres where it counts, and remember: the cheapest kilometre is always the one you cover safely, on time, and without burning more fuel than necessary. Keep it steady, keep it sensible, and the pump will thank you down the track.
