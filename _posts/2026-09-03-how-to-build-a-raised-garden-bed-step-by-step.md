### Choosing Your Bed Material: Budget vs. Durability

The first decision is what your frame will be made of. In Australia, we have some unique climate challenges, so material choice matters more than just the upfront price tag. Let's look at what you're getting for your dollar in 2026.

**Timber Frames**
Treated pine is the classic Aussie garden bed material. It warms up beautifully in our sun and gives that traditional cottage feel. However, timber prices have fluctuated recently. A robust **2m × 1m timber bed using quality H3 treated pine** will cost you approximately **$300 AUD**. This accounts for structural posts and boards that can handle the moisture cycle without warping too quickly. You can often find great deals on H3 pine at local timber yards, or check competitive rates online via [H3 Treated Pine Timber listings](https://www.amazon.com.au/s?k=H3+Treated+Pine+Timber&tag=owlno-22).

**Plywood Options**
For a quick cash grab, you might see sheets of plywood for around **$15 AUD**. A 2m × 1m bed using four sheets would cost roughly **$60 AUD**. While this is incredibly cheap upfront, I have to be honest with you: plywood tends to delaminate and rot faster in the harsh Aussie summer UV and heavy rains. It's a great budget hack if you're on an absolute shoestring or want a temporary solution for one season, but it won't last as long as timber.

**Concrete Block Beds**
If you want something that will outlive your grandchildren, concrete blocks are unbeatable. They're fireproof, rot-proof, and incredibly cheap. At **$5 AUD per block**, a 2m × 1m bed using just **12 blocks costs about $60 AUD**. The trade-off? They're heavy to stack and don't insulate roots quite as well as timber in winter, though the mass helps retain heat at night. This is a fantastic option for drought zones or fire-prone areas where longevity is key.

### Step-by-Step Build Guide

**Step 1: Site Selection and Preparation**
Choose a spot that gets at least six hours of sunlight daily. Avoid low-lying areas where cold air settles, as this can damage tender seedlings in winter. Clear the grass and weeds from your chosen area. If you're building directly on soil, lay down a layer of cardboard to smother existing grass without using chemicals. This is free and breaks down naturally, feeding your soil over time.

**Step 2: Assemble the Frame**
Lay your timber boards out on flat ground in the shape of your bed. If you're using concrete blocks, stack them according to your height plan. For timber, pre-drill holes at the corners to prevent splitting, then secure the corners with your outdoor screws. Use a spirit level as you go; shimming one corner with gravel or wood offcuts can correct uneven ground before you move the bed. Ensure the frame is square by measuring diagonals from opposite corners—they should be equal.

**Step 3: Position and Secure the Bed**
Carefully lift your assembled frame into position. If using timber, dig small holes at each corner post and set them in a bit of concrete or compacted gravel for extra stability against wind and shifting soil. For block beds, ensure each layer is level before adding the next. Once positioned, backfill the gap between the bed walls and the ground with soil or gravel to lock everything in place and prevent pests from nesting underneath.

**Step 4: Fill Your Bed with the Perfect Soil Mix**
This is where your harvest potential takes shape. A common mistake is filling the bed with just topsoil, which can compact over time. Instead, create a lightweight, nutrient-rich mix. A proven budget-friendly recipe is **50% quality topsoil, 30% organic compost, and 20% coarse sand or mulch**. Fill your bed to within 5cm of the rim. Water the soil heavily after filling to settle it, then top up the mix as it drops. This initial watering also helps compact the soil to a stable density for planting.

**Step 5: Mulch and Plan Your Irrigation**
Mulching is essential in Australia to retain moisture and suppress weeds. Apply a thick layer of straw or wood chips on top of your soil, keeping it slightly away from plant stems to prevent rot. ### FAQ: Common Raised Bed Questions for Aussie Gardeners

**Q1: Do I need drainage holes in my raised bed?**
A: In a raised bed sitting directly on soil, you generally don't need drilled holes; gravity handles the excess water by allowing it to seep into the ground below. However, if you're placing your bed on a patio, paved surface, or concrete slab, drainage becomes critical to prevent waterlogging and rot. Always ensure there's a gap underneath for airflow and use a layer of coarse gravel at the base to direct moisture away from your timbers when building on hard surfaces.

**Q2: What is the best soil mix for raised beds in 2026?**
A: You'll want a blend that retains moisture but drains well, especially with our erratic weather patterns and increasing temperatures. A proven budget-friendly recipe is 50% quality topsoil, 30% organic compost, and 20% coarse sand or perlite to improve aeration. Avoid using pure garden soil from your backyard if it's heavy clay, as it can compact over time and restrict root growth in a confined space. Adding a handful of slow-release fertiliser at planting time also supports healthy growth without the need for expensive liquid feeds.

**Q3: Can I use recycled pallets for my raised bed?**
A: While pallets are a popular zero-cost option, you must check the stamp on the wood to ensure it's heat-treated (HT) rather than chemically treated (MB). Only use pallets marked HT to avoid leaching harmful toxins like methyl bromide into your vegetable patch. If the pallets are in poor condition or show signs of rot, splintering, or chemical treatment, it’s best to skip them and opt for untreated cedar, redwood, or commercially rated garden lumber instead. Your vegetables—and your health—are worth the small investment.

**Q4: How deep should my raised bed be?**
A: For most vegetables, a depth of 12 to 18 inches is ideal. Root crops like carrots and parsnips will appreciate the extra room, while herbs and leafy greens do well in shallower beds. If you have mobility concerns or want to reduce bending, consider building your bed 24 to 30 inches deep—it’s one of the best ergonomic upgrades I’ve made to my garden.

**Q5: Do raised beds need drainage holes?**
A: Yes, absolutely. Even with a well-draining soil mix, water can pool at the bottom if there’s no exit. Drill or punch several ½-inch holes in the base every few inches. If you’re placing the bed directly on grass or turf, let the roots decompose naturally beneath it to create a natural drainage layer without smothering the surrounding lawn.
