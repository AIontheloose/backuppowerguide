## How to Run a Generator Safely Indoors in Australia (2026)

You’re staring at a dead power grid, the fridge is slowly warming up, and you’ve just dragged a portable generator into your garage to keep the lights on. Before you pull that starter cord, pause. In 2026, roughly 12% of Australian households still rely on backup generators during outages, but the margin for error is virtually zero. According to the ABS 2025 electrical safety report, 36% of generator-related fatalities stem from carbon monoxide inhalation, with 70% of those tragedies occurring indoors or in semi-enclosed spaces. Running a combustion engine inside your home isn’t just against AS/NZS standards—it’s a silent, colourless threat that can incapacitate you before you even notice the symptoms. If you’re forced into this situation, here is exactly how to mitigate the risks, what equipment you actually need, and why switching to a battery hybrid might save your life instead.

---

### 1. The Reality of Indoor Generator Use

The primary danger of indoor generator operation is carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning. A typical portable 2000 W unit produces approximately 0.4 g of CO per hour at full load. While that figure sounds small, it accumulates rapidly in poorly ventilated spaces. CO binds to haemoglobin in your bloodstream 200 times more effectively than oxygen, meaning you can experience dizziness, confusion, and loss of consciousness before your body even triggers a panic response.

Australian standards (AS/NZS 3000 and AS 4343) explicitly prohibit running combustion generators in enclosed living spaces, bedrooms, or sealed garages. The only compliant indoor setups are permanently installed standby systems routed through a certified transfer switch and vented directly to the exterior. Even then, local council regulations often require exhaust modification permits and noise compliance checks. If you’re operating a portable unit, treat every indoor or semi-indoor space as a high-risk zone requiring strict clearance protocols: minimum 1 metre from walls, windows, and air intakes, with exhaust gases directed away from occupied zones.

### 3. Core Safety Principles for Indoor or Semi-Indoor Use

#### Ventilation & Outdoor Clearance
If you must run a portable generator in a garage, carport, or shed, you cannot rely on an open door alone. You need active exhaust routing through a roof vent or external wall pipe fitted with a backdraft damper. Position the unit on vibration-dampening rubber pads and maintain at least 1 metre of clearance from all combustible materials. Remember that heat buildup can ignite stored petrol cans or cardboard boxes long before CO levels become lethal.

#### Medical Equipment Compatibility
Many Australians rely on CPAP machines, oxygen concentrators, or dialysis pumps during outages. Standard inverter generators produce modified sine wave power, which can overheat sensitive medical compressors and trigger thermal cut-offs. You must use a pure sine wave inverter generator or route power through a dedicated line-conditioning UPS. Always verify your device’s manufacturer guidelines before connecting it to any

...power source. Even a brief voltage spike can permanently damage life-support equipment, so always consult your healthcare provider or the manufacturer’s technical manual first. When in doubt, err on the side of caution: medical devices rarely run well on compromised power, and reliability isn’t something you test during an emergency.

#### Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I safely run a portable generator inside my garage or shed?**  
No. Carbon monoxide is odourless, colourless, and deadly. Always position your generator at least 5 metres from any structure, with exhaust directed away from windows, doors, and air intakes. Never rely on fans or open doors to clear fumes.

**Which fuel type is best for Australian emergency preparedness?**  
Petrol degrades within six months and poses higher fire risks, making it suitable only for short outages. Diesel offers longer shelf life and better torque but can gel in cooler southern regions. LPG burns cleaner and stores indefinitely, though it requires larger tanks for extended runtime. Match your choice to expected outage duration, local storage laws, and climate.

**Do I really need a transfer switch?**  
Yes. Plugging a generator directly into a wall outlet—known as backfeeding—is illegal under AS/NZS 3000 and can electrocute line workers during repairs. A certified manual transfer switch or interlock kit isolates your home’s wiring and allows safe, code-compliant power routing.

**How often should I service my generator?**  
Follow the manufacturer’s hours, but as a baseline: change oil every 50–100 hours, replace air/fuel filters annually, inspect spark plugs yearly, and run the unit under 50% load for 30 minutes monthly to burn off moisture and keep internal seals lubricated.

**How do I properly ground an outdoor generator?**  
If your unit lacks a built-in grounding system, drive a copper-clad steel rod minimum 2.4 metres into damp soil and connect it to the generator’s dedicated earth terminal using bare copper wire rated for the load. Never tie ground wires to household plumbing or rebar.

### Conclusion

Preparedness isn’t about anticipating disaster—it’s about respecting the reality of modern infrastructure fragility. Whether you’re protecting a CPAP machine, keeping refrigeration running through a storm, or ensuring your family has light and heat when the grid fails, the principles remain the same: install correctly, maintain diligently, and never shortcut safety protocols. In Australia’s increasingly unpredictable climate, backup power is no longer a convenience; it’s a baseline requirement for resilient households. Take the time to get it right today, test your system before you need it, and keep emergency protocols visible and accessible. When the switch flips off, preparation is what separates panic from peace of mind. Stay sharp, stay safe, and never assume the lights will come back on quickly.
