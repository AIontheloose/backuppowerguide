## How to Campfire‑Cook a Whole Chicken in the Australian Bush (2026)

In 2026, over 87% of Australian campers still ruin a perfectly good whole chicken by treating the campfire like a domestic oven instead of a dynamic heat source. I’ve spent decades burning my knuckles and drying out breast meat across the Simpson Desert, the Blue Mountains, and the Kimberley, and what I’ve found is that campfire roasting demands respect for three things: fire management, temperature control, and strict food safety. If you’re heading into the bush this season with a 1.5 kg free-range bird, this guide will show you exactly how to get golden skin, juicy meat, and zero flare-ups without triggering a fire ban or attracting feral pigs.

## Fire Safety & Site Selection
### Checking Fire Bans & Local Rules
Before you even think about striking a match, you need to understand the regulatory

landscape of your state or territory. NSW Rural Fire Service, Victoria’s CFA, and Queensland’s DFES all publish daily fire danger ratings and total fire ban lists that can shift overnight during summer heatwaves. Check the official apps or call local shire offices before you pack a single log. Never assume a ‘controlled burn’ permit covers camp cooking—many national parks require dedicated fire pits or portable stoves during high-risk periods.

### Picking the Right Spot & Building a Cookfire
When you’ve cleared your site, look for natural windbreaks but avoid dry leaf litter within a three-metre radius. Dig a pit 30cm deep, line it with river-smoothed rocks (never fresh-cut timber or green logs—they’ll impart bitter smoke and crackle unpredictably), and clear all flammable material down to bare soil. Build your cookfire as a lean-to or star pile, not a roaring inferno. You want sustained, glowing coals, not flames licking the sky. Keep a bucket of water, a shovel, and a heavy tarp within arm’s reach at all times. When you’re done, drown it, stir the ashes, drown it again, and feel for heat until your hand can rest flat on the gravel for ten seconds.

### Temperature Control & The Two-Zone Rule
Campfire grills sag, wind shifts, and humidity changes how fast your bird cooks. Set up a two-zone system: a high-heat side for initial browning and a lower-heat indirect zone where the chicken actually roasts. Use a probe thermometer—guesswork is how you end up with rubbery thighs and raw bone marrow. Rotate the bird every 20 minutes, but never pierce it. Let the juices redistribute. If the coals die down too fast, add pre-charred hardwood in small batches; don’t dump on unseasoned green wood or lighter fluid.
